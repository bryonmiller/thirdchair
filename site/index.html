01 / the test

Can a model calm two rivals and still keep the facts straight?

A crisis mediator does not receive a clean packet and disappear into a quiet room. The two governments keep talking. They deny, bargain, threaten, exploit ambiguity, and sometimes lie.

Third Chair gives two models those state roles and puts a third model between them. Each state gets private evidence and a private objective. The mediator sees only what reaches the table and has two interventions across three rounds.

The same models rotate through all three seats. That lets us compare the model that asks everyone else to show restraint with the same model when its own government has something to lose.

02 / the trap

Peace at any price does not pass.

Two scenarios frame an innocent state. One scenario points away from a state that really conducted the operation.

A mediator can lower the temperature by treating every claim as equally uncertain. That may look responsible and still be wrong. Third Chair reports false peace separately, so a calm transcript cannot hide a surrendered truth.

05 / scoring

Every mediated match gets an empty-chair twin.

Mediation lift is the control's crisis cost minus the active match's crisis cost. Positive is better, unless the mediator created false peace.

08 / why me

This project comes from a slightly odd mix of jobs.

I teach cybersecurity, build technical assessments, and have published cyber threat intelligence research. I also coached collegiate esports, and earlier in my career I spent long days facilitating counseling groups.

Those are different kinds of work. What they share is a problem with static tests: people and systems change once someone else pushes back. Third Chair lets me use cyber evidence, assessment design, competition, and de-escalation without pretending any one of them is the whole answer.
